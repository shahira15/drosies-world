Teacher and Parent Guide

Drosie's First Scientific Conference is a free, interactive activity in which learners investigate a question, interpret evidence, write an abstract, build a poster and practice presenting at a simulated conference. This guide explains what it teaches, how to use it, and what it does and does not claim.

Please read first. The fruit fly experiment in Path A is a simulation with made-up data. The conference, the schedule and every scientist in the hall are fictional. Certificates and badges show participation only.

Learning objectives

By the end, learners should be able to:

  1. Explain why scientists attend conferences.
  2. Tell a research question from a hypothesis and a prediction.
  3. Identify variables and experimental controls.
  4. Explain why scientists collect repeated measurements.
  5. Distinguish observations from interpretations.
  6. Organize and interpret research data.
  7. Recognize that uncertainty and limitations are a normal part of science.
  8. Write a basic scientific abstract.
  9. Build a scientific poster.
  10. Communicate findings clearly.
  11. Respond appropriately to scientific questions.
  12. Understand how feedback and revision improve communication.
  13. Recognize the importance of honesty and evidence.

The activity deliberately does not teach that research always confirms a hypothesis or gives a "significant" result. It does not calculate p-values or claim statistical significance.

Recommended age range

Designed for ages 7 to 12. Ages 7 to 8 will usually need an adult to read aloud and to help with typing, and may do best with Path A. Ages 10 to 12 can work independently and often enjoy Path B. Older learners can go deeper on limitations, replicates and alternative explanations, and on critiquing the fictional posters.

Suggested classroom use

  • Science unit (5 sessions): Session 1 Chapters 1 to 3. Session 2 Chapter 4. Session 3 Chapters 5 and 6. Session 4 Chapters 7 and 8. Session 5 Chapters 9 and 10 and a real "poster walk" where learners present printed posters to each other.
  • Science fair alternative or companion: have learners do a real investigation first and use Path B to prepare their presentation.
  • Homeschool: one chapter or two per day. Use the discussion questions as conversation starters.
  • Science club or outreach (single session): do Path A Chapters 1 to 4, skim the abstract, and finish with the hall (Chapter 8).
  • Group work: pairs can share a device. One learner runs the trial, and the other counts and records. Then swap.

Guided or independent, Path A or Path B

  • Guided participation opens chapters in order. Each unlocks when the previous one is finished.
  • Independent participation lets learners open any chapter. Use it for older learners, for revisiting, or for teachers previewing.
  • Path A (simulated fruit fly research) gives every learner the same carefully built dataset, so a whole class can compare results. The simulated experiment compares a 25 °C group (control) with an 18 °C group: 10 flies each, 5 trials each.
  • Path B (my own research) is for a project the learner really did or is planning. The site never fills in data, results or conclusions for them. Observation and exploration projects need no hypothesis.

Explorer and Researcher levels, the map and the backpack

  • Explorer (about ages 6 to 9, provisional and not yet tested with children) uses pictures, tapping and short steps. Researcher (about ages 9 to 13) adds the full tables, text fields and numbers. Learners can switch at any time without losing work.
  • The map shows the ten places. The Research Backpack collects cards (question, prediction, methods, data, graph, findings, limits, abstract, poster, badge). A card is only collected when the learner has really done that piece of work.
  • Every picture activity has a keyboard or tap alternative to dragging, and the page can be used with reduced motion. The scientific content, the simulated fruit fly numbers and the completion rules are the same as before.

Chapter by chapter

1 · The Conference Invitation

Why scientists share work; badge; nickname; the point that conference work has usually not been through journal peer review.

  • Why might a scientist want to hear questions from other people?
  • What is the difference between sharing work at a conference and publishing in a journal?

2 · Choose Your Research Path

Hypothesis versus prediction; independent variable; the full study design.

  • Which sentence explains why, and which says what we expect to measure?
  • Why is it important to say this is a simulation?

3 · The Research Investigation

The climbing assay; sorting what changes, what is measured and what is kept the same; recording counts; variation.

  • Why aren't the five numbers identical?
  • Why do we keep the vial, the taps and the time of day the same?

4 · Become a Data Detective

Means, medians and ranges calculated from the learner's own recorded numbers; three graph types; a deliberately truncated axis.

  • Which graph shows every trial? Which one hides how much the trials vary?
  • What happens to the picture when a bar chart's axis starts at 3 instead of 0?
  • Is a statement an observation or an interpretation?
  • The five trials use the same 10 flies. Why are they not five independent replicates?

5 · The Abstract Studio

Five-part abstract with sentence starters and a feedback checker that compares the learner's numbers with their data and flags overclaiming.

  • Could a stranger understand your study from the abstract alone?
  • Which sentence would you change if you wanted to sound less sure?

6 · The Scientific Poster Studio

Three layouts, five color themes (contrast is computed), a figure made from the learner's own data, and printing or "Save as PDF".

  • What should a visitor notice first on your poster?
  • Are the axes and units on your figure clear?

7 · Registration and Preparation

A fictional check-in, program and board assignment; ordering a 60-second talk with arrow buttons (no dragging needed).

  • Why does the order "question, background, methods, results, meaning, limits" help listeners?

8 · The Interactive Conference Hall

Six areas to explore, five fictional scientists who ask questions, three fictional posters with a flaw each, and a fictional talk with claims to check.

  • What makes an answer to "Could another explanation fit?" stronger?
  • Which of the fictional posters would you trust least, and why?

9 · Present, Discuss, and Revise

A silent 60-second practice, an explanation challenge, three audience questions, a revision log and reflection. No microphone or camera is used.

  • When is "I don't know yet" a good scientific answer?
  • What did you change after thinking about the questions, and why?

10 · The Conference Finale

Certificate, printable abstract, poster and reflection, and a downloadable portfolio.

  • What would you investigate next, and what would you do differently?

Scientific terminology

The complete glossary is in the conference hall's Research Information Booth. Key terms: research question, hypothesis, prediction, independent variable, dependent variable, control (comparison) group, controlled variables, observation, interpretation, repeated measurements, independent replicate, uncertainty, limitation, correlation and causation, abstract, poster, peer review, simulation.

Assessment opportunities

The activity gives learners feedback, but it does not produce a validated measure of learning. Completing it does not prove that outcomes were met. The learner's own words are the best evidence. Things to look for:

  • Abstract: Does Results report only what was measured? Do the numbers match the data? Does the conclusion use careful language and name a limit?
  • Poster: Are axes labeled? Is the text short? Is the conclusion supported by the figure?
  • Hall answers and Chapter 9 answers: Can the learner say what the data cannot show? Can they propose a next experiment?
  • Revision log: Is each change explained with a reason related to the evidence?
  • Reflection: Is there evidence of thinking about how science works?

Learners can download a portfolio file (Chapter 10) to share with you. It contains everything they wrote.

Scientific accuracy notes

  • Simulated data. Path A's counts (25 °C: 7, 8, 6, 8, 7; 18 °C: 4, 5, 3, 5, 4) were written for teaching. Every drawing, graph, table, summary and score in the activity is computed from the same list, and tests check that they agree. They do not describe real fruit flies.
  • What the simulation says. It states that the cooler group had fewer flies above the line in this simulation and that this fits the prediction. It says that one vial per condition and repeated trials on the same flies mean we cannot say what all flies do or rule out differences between the groups.
  • No statistical tests. The activity reports means, medians and ranges. It does not calculate p-values or claim significance.
  • Established facts. "Fruit flies are ectotherms" and "flies tend to climb after being tapped to the bottom of a vial (negative geotaxis)" are standard biology. The two references below describe climbing assays. Please check a biology textbook for the background facts.
  • Feedback engine. Feedback on writing is produced by simple word-and-number pattern checks. It can miss a good answer and accept a weak one. Adults should read the learner's work as well.
  • Fictional scientists. They have not reviewed or verified anyone's work.

Privacy considerations

  • No login, ads, analytics or third-party tracking. No server receives anything the learner types.
  • Work is saved only in the browser's local storage on that device. It is lost if browsing data is cleared. Learners can use Back up my work to download a file and restore it later.
  • The site discourages real names, emails, web addresses and phone numbers in the nickname, author and thanks fields, and blocks the most obvious ones. It cannot catch everything, so please remind learners.
  • Shared devices: at the end of a session, use Back up my work then Erase my work, so the next user does not see it.
  • Downloaded portfolios, backups and printouts contain everything the learner wrote. Share them only with people you choose.

Accessibility considerations

Built in: keyboard operation (tested by an automated browser script), visible focus outlines, semantic headings and landmarks, labeled form fields, a skip link, larger-text and high-contrast settings, a setting and the device preference to reduce animation, text alternatives for the vial (a text description of every trial), every chart (an accessible description that lists its values, plus a table view for the graphs in Chapter 4), and button-based alternatives wherever another site might use dragging. Layouts have been checked for horizontal overflow at 320, 390, 768 and 1280 px.

What has not been verified: automated checks (axe-core) found no violations in the tested states, but automated tools find only part of the problems. The activity has not been tested by people using screen readers (such as NVDA, JAWS or VoiceOver), switch devices or voice control, and we make no claim of WCAG compliance. If you find a barrier, please tell us.

Standards alignment

This guide does not claim alignment with any set of educational standards. If you need to map the activity to your standards, please check the exact wording of those standards yourself.

Known limitations

  • Time estimates and age recommendations have not been piloted with learners.
  • The abstract, poster and answer checkers are word-pattern based.
  • Posters are exported through the browser's print dialog ("Save as PDF"). There is no separate PDF generator, so appearance depends on the browser and its print settings.
  • The activity is in English only.
  • Path A has one fixed dataset. Learners in the same class will get the same numbers.
