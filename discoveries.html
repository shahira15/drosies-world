Wait… Did That Fly Just MOONWALK?! 🌙

First, watch the REAL fruit flies! One walks forward. The other does something surprising. Can you spot it?

Real experimental footage credited to the Institute of Molecular Pathology (IMP) and Science/AAAS, shared on YouTube by Stefano Di Criscio. Watch on YouTube ↗. If the player is unavailable, use that link.

👀 Detective question: Did you see the second fly walking BACKWARD? Play it again and watch its tiny legs!

🪰 Tap, tap, tap… WHOA!

Imagine a little fruit fly exploring.

Tap. Tap. Tap. Six tiny legs carry her forward.

But what if she needs to go the OTHER way?

Back, back, BACK! How does her brain know what to do?

🧠 The mystery inside a tiny brain

Scientists asked that same question. They looked for special brain cells that help a fly change direction.

Brain cells can send messages to help a body move. They're called neurons. That's a big word for tiny messengers!

In 2014, scientists found some very important neurons. When they switched these neurons on in an experiment, the flies walked BACKWARD!

The scientists named them Moonwalker Descending Neurons. That's a mouthful, so we can call them MDNs.

🔬 But how could they be sure?

Scientists tried two clever tests. First, they activated the MDNs and watched the flies back up. Then they blocked messages from those cells. When the flies met a wall they couldn't pass, they had trouble backing away.

Two experiments. One exciting clue: these brain cells help control backward walking!

✨ The BIG discovery: Even a tiny fly has special brain cells that help it switch walking directions. Tiny brain. Six legs. Amazing science!

🕺 Your turn to moonwalk!

Ask a grown-up to help you find a clear space. Take three steps forward. Check behind you. Now take three careful steps backward.

Did your legs change direction? Your brain helped make that happen!

🔎 For curious grown-ups: the real science and sources

What the experiment showed: Artificial activation of MDNs triggered backward walking, while blocking their output interfered with backward walking at an impassable barrier. In the featured video, the experimental fly expressed a temperature-sensitive channel (TrpA1) and the arena was warmed to activate the targeted neurons. The name “Moonwalker” describes the neurons, not a new fly species.

Bidaye, S. S., Machacek, C., Wu, Y., & Dickson, B. J. (2014). Neuronal control of Drosophila walking direction. Science, 344, 97–101. Original paper ↗

Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center ↗ · FlyBase ↗. See the verified Moonwalker demonstration stock below.

🪰 Meet a real Moonwalker research stock!

Scientists have a special fruit fly stock that can walk backward when certain nerve cells are activated with red light. The flies need to be raised with a substance called retinal for this light-triggered response.

For scientists & educators: Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center #79596 (RRID:BDSC_79596). This stock expresses the light-sensitive protein CsChrimson in a subset of neurons. Intense 625 nm red light can trigger backward walking in appropriately prepared adult flies. It is a later teaching/research demonstration stock, not the exact fly genotype shown in the 2014 video.

Find BDSC stock #79596 ↗ · Vilinsky et al. (2018) reference ↗

🔬 Drosie’s Real Science Corner: Explore FlyBase!

For little scientists: Inside a fruit fly’s tiny brain are special messenger cells. Some are called Moonwalker Descending Neurons (MDNs). They help the fly take steps backward! FlyBase is like a giant science library where grown-ups can discover how researchers study these cells.

For teachers, older students & researchers: These verified records connect the original experiment, the neurons and the later Moonwalker demonstration stock.

  • Moonwalker descending neuron (MDN) ↗ — FlyBase anatomy term FBbt:00111308. The neuron type involved in initiating backward walking.
  • VT050660-GAL4 driver ↗ — FlyBase allele FBal0310103. Read the experimental evidence and see its connection to BDSC #79596. This driver also labels other neurons, so it is not exclusively MDN-specific.
  • VT050660-GAL4 insertion ↗ — FlyBase transgenic insertion FBti0169507, a genetic component of the demonstration stock.
  • Original 2014 discovery ↗ — FlyBase reference FBrf0224613, Bidaye and colleagues.
  • 2018 teaching study ↗ — FlyBase reference FBrf0240142, Vilinsky and colleagues.

Science note: “Moonwalker” is the name of a neuronal circuit, not a new species or a single gene. The red-light-sensitive demonstration stock and the temperature-activated flies in the original experiment use different methods.

DISCOVERY 002 · WHY FRUIT FLIES?

Why Do Scientists Love Tiny Fruit Flies? 🪰

Before we read, let's see what you already know! It's okay to guess.

🔍 Before You Read: Little Scientist Quiz

Pick one answer for each question. No names are collected.

🪰 Meet a tiny science superstar!

Imagine a fly so small it can sit on your fingertip. Why do scientists love studying it?

🌱 It grows up fast! A fruit fly can grow from an egg into an adult in about 10 days in a warm laboratory.

🥚 It has lots of babies! Scientists can study many flies in a short time.

🔬 It is easy to study! Flies are small, so scientists can keep many in the laboratory.

🧬 It shares important genes with us! Studying fly genes helps scientists ask questions about how living things grow, work, and sometimes get sick.

So the next time you see a tiny fruit fly, remember: little fly, BIG science!

🔬 For teachers & curious grown-ups: real science sources

Drosophila melanogaster is a widely used model organism because it has a short generation time, produces many offspring, is easy to maintain, and offers powerful genetic tools. At about 25°C, development from egg to adult commonly takes roughly 10 days; timing depends on conditions.
